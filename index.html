Rabbits inspire mixed reactions--frustration from gardeners, fixation from predators, affection from their guardians, adoration from 5th-graders.

If you are thinking of taking home a rabbit as a pet, consider a few facts:

  • Rabbits are social and need companionship. At the same time, their instincts as prey animals can make them skittish and fearful. If you want a pet you can pick up and hold, a domestic ("house rabbit") might not be the best fit.
  • Their upkeep can be pricey: They require specialized ("exotics") veterinarians, who charge two or three times as much as standard small-animal vets. Because rabbits are also prone to digestive issues, interruptions in their intake and output can escalate to G.I. stasis, a painful and potentially deadly condition that can require expensive surgery.
  • They must be supplied with fresh timothy hay, which they munch all day, and a salad of a few Romaine leaves with some parsley or cilantro twice daily.

However, bunny guardians report playful behavior--stacking plastic cups, climbing on their seated humans, performing repeated binkies (bursts in the air) during playtime. "Rabbity" people, as they are sometimes called, also delight in their charges' sudden flops, quiet companionship during hours of sedentary activity, sitting adjacent on the sofa while their humans read, scroll, or swipe, or watch TV or a streaming service. Newbies to the rabbit world are often astonished by the variety of breed, temperament, texture, size, pattern, and color.

This guide introduces the Great Northwest House Rabbit in all its glory, fragility, and quirkiness.
